Rigorous Evaluation of Determinization-Based Search, Game-Theoretic Alternatives, and Optimal Integration in Imperfect-Information Card GamesDeterminization and Perfect-Information Monte Carlo in Card GamesPerfect-Information Monte Carlo (PIMC) search is a popular online planning paradigm for games of imperfect information, particularly in card games where hidden states are defined by dealt hands. Formally, let $I$ be the current information set of the acting player, representing the set of all possible game states (or "worlds") $s \in I$ that are consistent with the player's private observations and the public history. PIMC operates by sampling a set of $N$ determinizations (concrete worlds) $D = \{s_1, s_2, \dots, s_N\}$ from a belief distribution over $I$. For each sampled world $s_i \in D$, PIMC invokes a perfect-information search algorithm (such as $\alpha\beta$-pruning or Monte Carlo Tree Search) to compute the optimal action-value $Q^*(s_i, a)$ for every legal action $a \in A(I)$. The actions are then aggregated across all worlds, typically by selecting the action that maximizes the average perfect-information value:$$a^* = \arg\max_{a \in A(I)} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N Q^*(s_i, a)$$PIMC is computationally appealing because it avoids the combinatorial explosion of searching directly over information sets, allowing the deployment of highly optimized perfect-information solvers.This paradigm has historically yielded world-class agents in trick-taking card games like Contract Bridge. For instance, Matthew Ginsberg’s GIB program utilized a double-dummy solver—a perfect-information search algorithm designed for bridge where all four hands are visible. To manage the immense search space of a full 52-card deal, GIB pioneered "partition search". This technique establishes equivalence classes of cards whose ranks are currently irrelevant to the game's outcome, reducing them to generic symbols and lowering the average branching factor to approximately 1.3, which corresponds to a highly searchable space of roughly 18,000 nodes per deal. GIB demonstrated the power of this approach by finishing twelfth in a field of thirty-four elite human experts at the 1998 Par Contest, establishing double-dummy solvers coupled with Monte Carlo sampling as the state-of-the-art approach for trick-taking domains.Despite its empirical successes in games like Bridge and Skat, PIMC suffers from two fundamental game-theoretic pathologies: strategy fusion and non-locality.Strategy FusionStrategy fusion arises because PIMC solves each sampled world independently, effectively assuming that the player will be able to make different decisions at future nodes depending on which world is actually active, even if those future nodes belong to the same information set. In a real imperfect-information game, the player must select a single, unified action for an entire information set. By solving each world independently, PIMC allows the future strategy of the player to "fuse" or adapt to the hidden information of that specific world, leading to a severe overestimation of the expected payoff.Consider a precise extensive-form game tree to illustrate strategy fusion:
At the root, the maximizing player (MAX) must choose between moving to the right to a guaranteed terminal node with a payoff of $1$ regardless of the active world, or moving to the left to a node that branches into World 1 and World 2.If MAX goes left, the opponent (MIN) chooses an action.In World 1, MIN can choose action $A$ (yielding a payoff of $1$ for MAX) or action $B$ (yielding $-1$).In World 2, MIN can choose action $A$ (yielding a payoff of $-1$ for MAX) or action $B$ (yielding $1$).Crucially, the nodes corresponding to MIN's choices in both worlds belong to the same collapsed information set; MIN cannot distinguish between World 1 and World 2 when deciding between $A$ and $B$.When standard PIMC evaluates this tree, it point-wise maximizes the payoffs in each world.For World 1, the solver assumes MAX will receive a payoff of $1$ by anticipating the favorable branch.For World 2, the solver assumes MAX will receive a payoff of $1$ by anticipating the alternative favorable branch.Averaging these values, PIMC estimates the expected payoff of going left as:$$\frac{1 + 1}{2} = 1$$PIMC evaluates both the left and right moves as wins. However, in real play, MIN is confused between the worlds and cannot make a world-specific decision. If MIN plays a Nash equilibrium strategy, the true expected payoff of going left is:$$\frac{1 + (-1)}{2} = 0$$MAX's choice to go left based on PIMC's recommendation results in a suboptimal decision due to strategy fusion, as PIMC incorrectly assumes the players can resolve their uncertainty at future nodes.Non-LocalityNon-locality refers to the property of imperfect-information games where the value of a specific node cannot be determined solely by searching its local subtree. In perfect-information games, the minimax value of a node is completely determined by its children. In imperfect-information games, a node's value depends on the probability distribution over the histories in the information set, which is a function of the global strategies of all players leading to that node.Because PIMC solves isolated perfect-information subtrees, it ignores this global strategic coupling. For example, PIMC is highly susceptible to the overestimation of the opponent's (MIN's) knowledge, as it implicitly operates under a "best defense model" where MIN is assumed to be clairvoyant. To mitigate this overestimation, variants such as Presumed Payoff PIMC and Presumed Value PIMC adjust the evaluation by point-wise penalizing payoff vectors in terminal nodes to reflect the opponent's actual lack of information, though these heuristics slow down the search significantly.PIMC succeeds primarily in games where:Information is revealed rapidly and irreversibly over the course of play (as in trick-taking games, where played cards continuously collapse the information set).The game has a high tactical density, meaning that deep tactical calculation under perfect-information assumptions outweighs the strategic errors introduced by strategy fusion and non-locality.Information-Set Monte Carlo Tree SearchTo mitigate the theoretical flaws of PIMC, Peter Cowling, Edward Powley, and Daniel Whitehouse introduced Information-Set Monte Carlo Tree Search (IS-MCTS). Rather than constructing separate search trees for each individual determinization, IS-MCTS builds a single, unified search tree where the nodes represent information sets from the perspective of the searching player.

Reduction of Strategy FusionBy forcing the search tree to represent information sets rather than explicit states, IS-MCTS guarantees that the planning player can only select a single action per information set at any point in the search. The algorithm groups statistics (visit counts and total rewards) at the level of the information set. Consequently, the selection policy cannot select different actions for different hidden states at a decision node, effectively eliminating strategy fusion for the player conducting the search.Redeterminization per SimulationThe core mechanism of IS-MCTS is to sample a new determinization at the beginning of each simulation (or search iteration). The simulation then proceeds down the single information-set tree, but the available legal actions and state transitions are strictly governed by the specific world sampled for that iteration. If a node in the tree is reached where the current player has no legal actions under the sampled determinization, or if the history is inconsistent with the sampled world, the simulation is redirected or the node is bypassed. This continuous redeterminization ensures that the tree statistics reflect the expected utility across the entire distribution of consistent worlds, rather than a single fixed world.Multiple-Observer and Advanced VariantsThe representation of opponent decisions divides the IS-MCTS family into distinct algorithmic structures:Single-Observer IS-MCTS (SO-ISMCTS): This variant searches a single tree corresponding to the information sets of the root player, assuming that all opponent actions are fully observable. It is highly effective in card games where played cards are placed face-up, meaning players can see each other's moves.SO-ISMCTS with Partially Observable Moves (SO-ISMCTS+POM): This variant extends the single-observer model to games where some details of the actions performed by the opponent are hidden from the planning player.Multiple-Observer IS-MCTS (MO-ISMCTS): This variant constructs and searches a separate tree for each player simultaneously. It is designed for games with partially observable moves where players cannot fully observe the other players' or teams' moves, ensuring that private information is not leaked through the search structure.Multiple-Tree IS-MCTS (MT-ISMCTS): This variant addresses the limitations of SO-ISMCTS regarding opponent modeling and bluffing. MT-ISMCTS maintains one tree per opponent for each information set they could be in. It updates these trees using "fake" determinizations—states the acting player knows to be incorrect but which could be correct from the opponent's perspective—allowing the agent to simulate how its actions mislead the opponent and to execute effective bluffs.Practical Knobs for OptimizationExploration Constant ($C$): The UCT selection formula must use a modified visit count $N(v)$ representing the number of times the parent node was visited and the child node was available for selection under the active determinization.Information Leakage Management: Running MCTS on information sets can implicitly leak game state information during playouts. If the playout policies are allowed to adapt across different iterations, they may implicitly converge to "knowing" the hidden cards. To prevent this, the playout policies must remain static or be restricted to legal observations.Belief-Based and Bayesian Determinization in Deep Reinforcement LearningModern deep reinforcement learning (DRL) architectures have scaled search and learning in highly complex, imperfect-information card games by combining neural belief models with modified planning algorithms.DeltaDou (DouDizhu)DeltaDou achieves human expert-level performance in the three-player card game DouDizhu by integrating deep self-play with Bayesian hand inference and asymmetric MCTS.Handling of the Fusion Problem: DeltaDou utilizes Fictitious Play MCTS (FPMCTS), which builds a search tree of information sets for each player. To completely avoid strategy fusion and the exponential growth of the search tree, DeltaDou treats opponent moves as chance nodes. At these chance nodes, instead of searching recursively, opponent actions are sampled directly from their respective learned policy networks. This restricts the search depth and ensures that opponent actions are guided by realistic, localized strategies that do not "leak" the player's private hand.Inference Engine: DeltaDou runs a policy-guided Bayesian inference algorithm at the root of the search to sample plausible hands for the opponents based on the public history of play. This is conditionally activated when a player has fewer than 15 cards, enabling highly accurate, localized determinizations in the endgame.Suphx (Mahjong)Suphx represents a landmark achievement in Mahjong, a multi-player game with massive hidden information ($10^{48}$ hidden states per information set) and irregular rules that prevent traditional MCTS.Oracle Guiding: During reinforcement learning training, Suphx uses an "oracle" critic that has access to perfect information (including other players' private tiles and the wall tiles). The policy is regularized to distill this perfect-information knowledge, which is gradually phased out until the execution policy takes only imperfect observations as input.Parametric Monte Carlo Policy Adaptation (pMCPA): Because of the irregular game tree, Suphx avoids online search altogether at execution time. Instead, it uses pMCPA, which online-adapts the offline policy network parameters to the specific trajectory of the active round as more public cards are revealed, effectively tuning the policy parameters to the specific belief state of the online game.ReBeL (Recursive Belief-Based Learning)ReBeL generalizes AlphaZero to imperfect-information games by reformulating the extensive-form game as a perfect-information game played over Public Belief States (PBS).Public Belief States: A PBS $\beta = (s_{pub}, \Delta S_1, \Delta S_2)$ represents the public state $s_{pub}$ (the sequence of public cards and actions) combined with the joint probability distributions $\Delta S_i$ over each player's private infostates. Because all players can compute the same $\beta$ using Bayes' rule and an agreed-upon blueprint strategy, the PBS is common knowledge.Search over PBS: ReBeL performs depth-limited search (CFR) directly over the space of PBSs, utilizing a value network to predict the minimax value of the leaf PBSs. Because there are no information sets at the belief state level, strategy fusion is mathematically eliminated. It achieves superhuman performance in heads-up no-limit Texas Hold'em but requires immense training compute.Player of GamesPlayer of Games (PoG) is a unified, sound search algorithm designed to perform across both perfect and imperfect-information domains.Growing-Tree CFR (GT-CFR): PoG uses GT-CFR as its core search engine. It dynamically grows a search tree over public belief states, resolving subgames online while using deep value networks to approximate the counterfactual values of the frontier. Like ReBeL, PoG's grounding in PBS transitions guarantees game-theoretic soundness and eliminates the strategy fusion pathology entirely, though it is highly computationally intensive.The Counterfactual Regret / Game-Theoretic AlternativeThe game-theoretic alternative to determinization is Counterfactual Regret Minimization (CFR), which is the standard framework for solving imperfect-information extensive-form games.Soundness of CFR/PBS MethodsCFR-based search over Public Belief States (as implemented in ReBeL and DeepStack) is theoretically sound because it computes a localized approximation of a Nash equilibrium. Standard PIMC is highly exploitable because it assumes a "clairvoyant" player who can tailor their action to each individual world, ignoring the constraint that they must play the same action across the information set. CFR mathematically guarantees that the computed strategy converges to a Nash equilibrium, preventing the opponent from exploiting any information-set inconsistencies.The counterfactual value (CFV) of an information set $I$ for player $p$ under a strategy profile $\sigma$ is defined as the expected utility given that $I$ has been reached, assuming all players follow $\sigma$ except that player $p$ plays to reach $I$ with certainty :$$v^{\sigma}_p(I) = \sum_{h \in I} \left( \pi^{\sigma}_{-p}(h) \sum_{z \in Z, h \sqsubseteq z} \pi^{\sigma}(z|h) u_p(z) \right)$$By minimizing regrets defined over these counterfactual values, CFR ensures that the average strategy converges to a Nash equilibrium in two-player zero-sum games.Computational Cost of CFR vs. PIMCAdopting CFR/PBS methods introduces a Mechanism of PTIEDuring the training phase, the system has access to perfect information (the exact hands of all players). A centralized value network (the critic) takes this perfect information as input to calculate highly accurate state-value or action-value estimates. The policy network (the actor), however, is strictly restricted to imperfect information (only the player's own hand and the public history).The actor is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). The perfect-information critic provides low-variance, "unbiased" advantages to guide the actor's policy updates, effectively distilling the strategic insights of a perfect-information "cheater" into an actor that can only execute using legal, imperfect-information observations.FPDou's RefinementsRecent advancements under the FPDou framework have refined this training paradigm. In the card game DouDizhu (a three-player game involving asymmetric roles), FPDou treats the two cooperating Peasant players as a single unified agent, reducing the environment to a two-player zero-sum formulation amenable to Fictitious Play analysis. FPDou addresses the training-execution representation gap by introducing an $L_2$ regularization penalty to minimize the discrepancy between the latent representations of the perfect-information and imperfect-information network passes:$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}} = \lambda \left\| z_{\text{perfect}} - z_{\text{imperfect}} \right\|_2^2$$This latent feature regularization forces the imperfect-information actor to implicitly learn global cooperative intent and card distribution inferences from local observations. Furthermore, FPDou adopts an alternating on-policy/off-policy updates training scheme. By freezing the opponent's strategy during best-response training and utilizing an off-policy copy to sample trajectories, it stabilizes the non-stationary self-play environment while maximizing sample efficiency. In terms of card representation, these models typically encode hands and legal combinations as a multi-dimensional matrix (such as a $12 \times 15$ feature tensor) to represent ranks, combinations (singles, pairs, chains, bombs), and unplayed cards.Comparison with Determinized MCTSPTIE completely avoids strategy fusion and non-locality because the actor network is structurally constrained to select actions based only on the local information set. It features sub-millisecond inference times, making it highly suitable for online deployment.However, PTIE has a major drawback: it is a "blind" policy. Because it does not run any online lookahead search, it cannot "think" through complex, novel tactical sequences or guarantee wins in deep endgame scenarios where an exact solver could calculate a forced win. In a highly tactical game like Big 2, a pure PTIE agent will frequently make tactical blunders in the late game that a shallow search would easily detect.Aggregation Paradigms for Determinized SearchWhen integrating a perfect-information evaluator with a belief model, the choice of aggregation function at the root of the search is critical to managing bias and strategy fusion.1. Averaging Per-World Optimal Value (Average-of-Max / Standard PIMC)This is the standard PIMC approach. The value of an information set $I$ is estimated by averaging the maximized perfect-information values across the sampled worlds :$$V_{\text{AoM}}(I) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \max_{a \in A(I)} Q_{\text{PI}}(s_i, a)$$Bias: Extremely optimistic and highly susceptible to strategy fusion. It assumes the player can magically select the optimal action $a$ tailored to the specific world $s_i$ at the root, even if those actions differ across worlds.Effect: Leads to highly aggressive, over-optimistic play and an inability to value information gathering or defensive maneuvers.2. Averaging a Single Committed Policy (Max-of-Average / Root-Commitment)To eliminate strategy fusion at the root, the player is forced to commit to a single, unified action across all sampled worlds :$$V_{\text{MoA}}(I) = \max_{a \in A(I)} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N Q_{\text{PI}}(s_i, a)$$Bias: Unbiased at the root. It strictly respects the information-set constraint for the immediate decision point. However, it still suffers from downstream strategy fusion because the perfect-information evaluator $Q_{\text{PI}}(s_i, a)$ is evaluated under the assumption that the player will make optimal, "fused" perfect-information choices at all subsequent moves.Effect: Yields a much sharper, more useful search value than standard PIMC, successfully preventing immediate "cheating" behavior at the root while leveraging the tactical depth of the perfect-information evaluator.3. Principled Middle GroundsTo manage the remaining downstream strategy fusion in the perfect-information evaluator, three advanced aggregation and search structures are available:Extended PIMC (EPIMC) with Postponed ReasoningIntroduced by Jérôme Arjonilla, Abdallah Saffidine, and Tristan Cazenave, EPIMC postpones the invocation of the perfect-information leaf evaluator to a depth $d$ in the search tree. From the root up to depth $d$, the agent searches an imperfect-information tree (using the user's imperfect-information policy network). Only at the frontier nodes of depth $d$ does the algorithm sample $N$ worlds and invoke the perfect-information evaluator. This strictly reduces strategy fusion, with strategy fusion completely eliminated if $d$ equals the game's depth.Local Regret-Matching / Fictitious Play across WorldsInstead of simple averaging, a mini-CFR or Fictitious Play algorithm is run online specifically over the $N$ sampled worlds at the root. The search maintains the constraint that the acting player must choose the same action distribution across all $N$ worlds, while the opponent (who is modeled as having perfect information or a distinct belief) can select different actions. This converts the root decision into a small, tractable matrix game, computing a robust, local Nash equilibrium strategy that explicitly handles bluffing and defense.Vector-Value Backups (Fusing Only at Frontiers)During the MCTS search, the tree maintains a single action path for the player, but backs up a vector of values of length $N$ (one value per sampled world). The selection policy at the root uses the average of these vectors. This ensures that the policy updates are driven by a single, committed strategy across all simulations, while preserving the sharp, world-specific feedback from the perfect-information evaluator at the leaves.Practical Parameterization and Engineering GuidelinesTo implement a highly performant, belief-guided search under strict computation budgets, the following empirical parameters and guidelines should be followed:1. Belief Model Calibration and SensitivityThe performance of belief-based determinization is highly sensitive to the calibration of the posterior distribution.Out-of-Distribution Sensitivity: If the belief model assigns zero probability to the opponent's true hand (due to unexpected opponent play or "thermo" constraint violations), the determinization sampler will fail to generate consistent worlds.Uncalibrated Beliefs: If the belief model is overly confident (low entropy, but wrong), the search will confidently commit to catastrophic tactical moves.Mitigation: A mixture model should be used. The sampled hands should be drawn from:$$P_{\text{sample}} = (1 - \gamma) P_{\text{Belief}} + \gamma P_{\text{Uniform}}$$where $\gamma \in [0.05, 0.15]$ is a uniform noise term injected to ensure robustness against deceptive opponent plays (bluffs).3. Fix-at-Root vs. Redeterminize-per-SimulationFix-at-Root (Standard PIMC): Sample $N$ worlds at the root. Run $N$ independent MCTS runs. This is highly parallelizable (each world can be solved on a separate CPU thread or GPU stream). However, it does not share information between trees and suffers heavily from strategy fusion.Redeterminize-per-Simulation (IS-MCTS): Maintain a single tree. Sample a new world for each MCTS iteration. This is highly computationally efficient (requires only one MCTS run). However, in a deep neural network setting, redeterminization per simulation is difficult to batch efficiently on GPUs, leading to severe latency bottlenecks.Recommendation: Use Fix-at-Root with Root-Commitment Aggregation. Sample $N$ worlds, perform a shallow perfect-information search or policy query for each, and aggregate using the Max-of-Average formula.
