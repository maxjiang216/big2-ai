# Determinization / Perfect-Information Monte Carlo (PIMC)

Determinization (aka Perfect-Information Monte Carlo) means sampling hidden states (“worlds”) consistent with the current information set and solving each as a perfect-information game.  In practice one takes many samples of the opponent’s hand (from a belief distribution) and runs a perfect-information search (e.g. MCTS or alpha-beta) in each, then **averages** the resulting action-values.  PIMC has been surprisingly effective in some large games (e.g. Bridge GIB) even though it is theoretically flawed.  Its key failure modes were identified by Frank & Basin (1998): **strategy fusion** and **non-locality**. Strategy fusion means PIMC assumes you can play differently in different sampled worlds even when you can’t distinguish them – i.e. it “fuses” separate strategies across a single information set.  Non-locality means that in an imperfect-information game a subtree’s value depends on hidden information elsewhere (so optimal play can depend on far-away belief), which PIMC ignores. In effect, PIMC often gives an **optimistic** bias (choosing actions that might win in at least one world) and ignores adversarial inference.  

Long *et al.* (2010) formalized this: they showed PIMC tends to work when the “leaf nodes” of the sampled subtrees are highly correlated across worlds, but can fail badly otherwise.  In practice, simple tricks (e.g. pruning low-probability deals, correlating deals to heuristic values) are used to improve it (as in Bridge GIB).  But the core problems remain: PIMC’s value estimates regress toward the *average* payoff (the “mushy” expectation) and high-variance outliers can dominate.  In summary, PIMC/determinization is easy to implement and has low requirements, but is **biased/unsound** (no Nash guarantee) and susceptible to strategy fusion and non-locality.  

## Information-Set MCTS (IS-MCTS)

Info-set MCTS methods aim to mitigate fusion by *sharing* search across samples.  Cowling, Powley & Whitehouse (2012) introduced **ISMCTS**, which builds a single search tree whose nodes are information-sets rather than fully determined states.  In practice, each simulation **redeterminizes** at the root (samples a world), then proceeds with MCTS, but any time two samples reach the *same* information set of the same player, they use the *same node* in the tree.  This forces the algorithm to pick a single action per information set (it cannot “cheat” by choosing differently in different samples).  In effect, the UCT statistics for each action are aggregated over all sampled worlds, dramatically reducing strategy fusion.

Two main variants were proposed. In **Single-Observer ISMCTS (SO-ISMCTS)** the tree is built from the perspective of a single root player: nodes correspond to that player’s infosets and edges to actions (even opponent actions), and opponent moves are treated as unobservable (collapsing them so the root player sees just one branch).  After many iterations, SO-ISMCTS’s value estimates become consistent with the fact that the root player cannot distinguish worlds.  In **Multi-Observer ISMCTS (MO-ISMCTS)**, the algorithm effectively builds *two* trees, one from each player’s perspective, accounting for what each knows after an opponent move.  MO-ISMCTS further prevents fusion caused by treating an opponent’s hidden moves as known. 

In summary, ISMCTS (especially MO-ISMCTS) uses redeterminization per simulation and shares one tree across all samples.  This does **not** fully solve non-locality (the opponent still effectively plays random), but it **guarantees no strategy fusion**: the algorithm never assumes a player can distinguish two states in the same information set.  The trade-off is higher branching and the need to allocate search to one bigger tree (so fewer plies searched per node).  Empirically, ISMCTS often outperforms naïve PIMC/UCT in games where deeper search matters more than hidden info (e.g. Lord-of-the-Rings: Confrontation).  In trick-taking games, the branching factor can blow up, so the advantage is less clear.  In short: **ISMCTS is partially sound** (no fusion) but still heuristic (no Nash guarantee), requires redeterminization and larger trees, and can search shallower per iteration.

## Belief-based Deep-RL Card Agents

Recent AI agents for large card games have combined deep learning with belief sampling to avoid fusion.  **DeltaDou** (Jiang *et al.*, IJCAI 2019) tackled DouDizhu (a 3p Big-2-like game) using an *AlphaZero*-style pipeline.  Crucially, it introduced **Fictitious-Play MCTS (FPMCTS)**: an asymmetric MCTS where *only the current player’s moves* are chosen by search (PUCT with a value/policy net), while *other players’ moves* are sampled from their neural policy nets.  As the authors note, sampling opponents from a fixed policy “avoids the issues of strategy fusion and non-locality”.  (Intuitively, it fixes each turn to one choice and models opponents probabilistically.)  DeltaDou also implemented a Bayesian inference step: it generates candidate opponent hands and weights them by how well they explain the neural policy’s observed moves.  In practice this produced an expert-level DouDizhu player comparable to top humans.  In our context, FPMCTS is like combining ISMCTS (fixed moves for others) with belief sampling, letting each simulation focus on one determinized world’s best play. 

**Suphx** (Li *et al.*, 2020) is a Mahjong AI that also uses deep RL with sampling.  It did *not* run MCTS at inference, but instead used a clever **parametric Monte-Carlo policy adaptation (pMCPA)**.  At the start of each round (hand), Suphx samples many complete deals from the player’s belief (own hidden tiles are known, others’ and wall drawn tiles are sampled randomly), then it rolls out its offline-trained policy network on each sample.  The resulting trajectories are used to fine-tune (by gradient descent) the policy net **for this round only**.  In effect, it does Monte-Carlo simulations from multiple worlds *to adapt the policy*, not to choose a single search action.  This boosts performance significantly in Mahjong.  Suphx’s method is “Bayesian” in spirit (sampling worlds from a prior) but it sidesteps fusion by gradient-updating a single policy rather than merging multiple action outcomes.  Note it uses no search at execution time—only policy updates on the fly. 

**ReBeL** (Brown *et al.*, NeurIPS 2020) and **Student/Player of Games** (Schmid *et al.*, Science 2023) take a different approach: they **expand the state space to include belief distributions** and use CFR-style solving.  ReBeL frames each “state” as a public belief state (the distribution over hidden cards consistent with history) and uses deep value/policy networks trained by self-play.  Search is then done by *depth-limited recursive reasoning*: at a leaf of search, they run a CFR sub-solver on the public belief subgame (using the networks for evaluation) to compute values and next-policy updates.  This method is *provably sound*: ReBeL converges to a Nash equilibrium in 2p zero-sum games as compute grows.  Similarly, the Student/Player of Games (SoG/PoG) algorithm grows a public belief game tree on the fly and runs a “Growing-tree CFR” (GT-CFR) search every move.  GT-CFR alternates CFR updates and selective node expansions under the current public state, yielding policies that approach equilibrium (theorems in the paper bound exploitability).  These methods **completely avoid strategy fusion** by construction, but at the cost of heavy CFR computations (deep regret updates, value networks, etc.).  They have achieved strong results (e.g. superhuman Heads-Up Poker, and strong play in 2-4p games), but require substantial training and per-move search. 

In summary, modern deep RL card AIs handle fusion in different ways: DeltaDou/FPMCTS and Suphx use belief sampling plus either search or online adaptation, accepting some bias but achieving good play.  ReBeL/Player-of-Games use **theoretically-grounded public-belief-state CFR** search, which is unbiased (sound) but costly. 

## CFR / Public-Belief-State (PBS) Methods

A counterpoint to sampling-based heuristics is game-theoretic **regret minimization**.  Counterfactual Regret Minimization (CFR) and its variants (e.g. DeepCFR, CFR+) directly seek equilibrium strategies in 2-player zero-sum games.  In an imperfect-info setting, one typically works in the **public belief/state space** (common knowledge state plus beliefs) and updates regrets for each information set.  These methods are *provably convergent* to Nash: e.g. ReBeL (Brown *et al.* 2020) “provably converges to a Nash equilibrium in any two-player zero-sum game” by treating each belief-augmented state as an EF abstraction.  

**Soundness vs. bias:** In contrast to PIMC’s optimism, CFR-based approaches are unbiased: they minimize regret in expectation, so the learned strategy is robust (minimax).  This means no strategy fusion occurs and no unrealistic information assumptions are made.  However, the cost is high.  You must run iterative regret updates or on-the-fly solving for each node. For example, Student-of-Games’s GT-CFR invokes a CFR solve at every move, expanding the belief tree gradually.  The computational complexity can be quadratic or worse in the number of leaf expansions.  Even ReBeL, which offloads CFR into deep nets, required training value/regret networks on millions of poker hands.  Adopting CFR in your setting means implementing an entire regret-minimizing solver or adapting a library, plus training networks that evaluate belief states.  

**Is it worth it?** For a 2p zero-sum game, CFR/PBS is the *theoretically ideal* path (Nash solution), and it **eliminates strategy-fusion bias** entirely.  If your application absolutely requires equilibrium-level play, then yes.  However, the implementation and compute cost can be prohibitive compared to determinization.  Many practical card AIs achieve strong play with heuristics (DeltaDou, Suphx, Oracle methods) without full CFR.  In our case, if cheaper methods (like belief-sampled MCTS) already reach acceptable strength, a switch to CFR might be overkill.  On the other hand, if value misestimation (“mushiness”) persists and harming performance, CFR-inspired search (like ReBeL-style or GT-CFR) would guarantee improvement – at the price of major engineering and training effort.

## Perfect-Training-Imperfect-Execution (PTIE)

Perfect-Training-Imperfect-Execution (PTIE) or **perfect-information distillation** is a recent idea to avoid fusion altogether by learning from omniscient critics.  Yang *et al.* (NeurIPS 2022) applied this to DouDizhu with *PerfectDou*.  They train an actor-critic system with **global (perfect) information** during training, but constrain the actor to use only observed info at execution.  Concretely, a perfect-information value network (critic) helps compute advantages, while the policy network (actor) only sees the information-set features. Training uses PPO with GAE in parallel games.  

The result is a policy that was guided by perfect info during learning, but at test time behaves solely on partial info (no search, no multiple moves).  This sidesteps strategy fusion by never relying on hidden info at execution; effectively the actor learns a robust policy averaged over the hidden states seen during training.  In practice, PerfectDou achieved state-of-the-art play in DouDizhu without any online search. 

**Comparison to determinized-MCTS:** PTIE avoids mushy values by not using them at search: it produces a single action from the learned policy.  It also sidesteps fusion issues by training on full info (so the actor has implicitly learned the “value of information”).  The downsides are that it requires a two-network RL training (like SAC or PPO) and still may suffer from some averaging if the policy cannot differentiate subtle scenarios.  It also means **no search** at inference, so if your perfect-evaluation module is strong, this approach doesn’t exploit it.  For our case (we have a strong perfect-info MCTS and value net), PTIE would throw away that search power at runtime.  It might serve as a “no-search baseline” to try if search-based fixes fail, but by itself it will not leverage the perfect-info solver you have.  

## Aggregating Multiple Determinizations

A practical issue in determinization methods is how to **combine** results from different sampled worlds.  The two extremes are (a) “optimistic” value-averaging and (b) “pessimistic” fixed-policy averaging.  

- **Optimistic (per-world best):** One approach is to run a *full* perfect-information solution in each world (selecting the best action there) and then average those values.  This effectively assumes one can adapt to the world, yielding an upper-biased value.  It often prefers high-variance actions that win big in some worlds.  This is what naïve PIMC/UCT does if you pick max-Q in each tree and average.  

- **Conservative (fixed strategy):** Alternatively, you could decide on one strategy ahead of time and evaluate it across worlds (i.e. for each candidate action, compute its expected value under the belief).  This is the *unbiased* expected value of a single mixed strategy (it’s just the belief-weighted average of outcomes).  This is what a Bayesian expected utility policy does.  In practice this yields the “mushy” 0.5-ish values when actions have roughly equal average payoffs, as seen in the user’s scenario.  

Is there a middle ground?  One could imagine weighting worlds differently or incorporating “regret-matching” across them.  For example, one might run a regret-minimization across sampled worlds: treat each determinization as a scenario and find a strategy that minimizes regret across all scenarios.  This essentially blends PIMC with CFR.  In fact, Online Outcome Sampling (OOS) or Counterfactual Regret Search can be seen as doing this in an online way.  Another idea is to use a max-weight backup (like backup the best child’s value if that is consistent across many worlds).  However, no clean “half-optimistic” rule is standard; most implementations simply average action-values (implicitly picking a *single* action by highest average) or take a majority vote move.  

In short, the principled approach to avoid both extremes is to **explicitly solve for a strategy**, e.g. via regret minimization.  Absent that, one might try heuristics: e.g. weight each sample by its posterior probability (Bayesian averaging), or partially “prune” low-value worlds before averaging.  But any ad-hoc fusion can introduce bias.  The safest method in this category remains *one-action-per-infoset*, as in ISMCTS or FPMCTS, so that the search does not average conflicting chosen actions across worlds.  

## Practical Parameters and Trade-offs

**Number of determinizations (N):**  The required N depends on variance.  In practice, bridge AIs (e.g. GIB) use on the order of 50–100 deals per move.  More samples reduce statistical noise but increase cost linearly.  For 16-card hands vs 52-card bridge, you may need fewer samples, but if the belief model is poor, more samples just wastes time on wrong worlds.  A reasonable starting point might be *tens of samples per decision*, evaluating whether results converge.  

**Belief calibration:**  If your belief sampler is miscalibrated (wrong probabilities), determinization will be biased.  Poor beliefs can make a search think an unlikely world is likely, skewing decisions.  It’s important to test sensitivity: try injecting noise into beliefs and see how values/actions change.  In some cases one might temper the belief (flatten probabilities) or mix with a uniform prior to guard against overconfidence.  

**Redeterminize-per-simulation vs fix-root:**  In Monte Carlo Tree Search, a common choice is to redeterminize the world at the start of *each simulation* (as in POMCP style).  This introduces variability in deeper nodes as well.  Alternatively, one could sample once at the root and then follow that world fully to terminal (“fix at root”).  Redeterminizing every move can better explore the belief space but may reintroduce partial observability bias (non-locality), whereas fixing the world per simulation treats later chance moves as known, possibly underestimating opponent inference.  In practice, **redeterminizing at each node** (common in ISMCTS and POMCP) is safer: each simulated path is a plausible full trajectory.  

**Compute trade-offs:**  Determinizations multiply cost: e.g. 100 samples each with 500 MCTS sims is 50K total rollouts.  If your PI evaluator is very fast, this may be fine; if not, you may need to reduce samples or MCTS depth.  One tactic is *progressive widening*: start with few worlds and focus more simulations on promising ones.  Or use *root-parallel MCTS*: run many determinized MCTS trees in parallel and merge their statistics.  

Overall, there is no one-size-fits-all.  A good approach is iterative: start with a modest N and observe variance in move-values; increase N until choices stabilize.  Monitor the belief model: if it systematically underestimates opponent strength, consider mixing in pessimistic scenarios.  

## Method Comparison

|--------------------------------|----------------------|--------------------------------|-------------|---------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Naïve PIMC (fix-root)**      | Biased (optimistic)  | None (fusion occurs)           | Low         | Yes                 | Simple to try, will likely be biased and give mushy values. |

| **pMCPA (Suphx-style)**        | Biased (approx)      | No fusion (single policy)      | Moderate    | **No** (policy update)| Uses belief sampling to fine-tune policy; no search runtime cost, but reliant on RL setup. |

## Recommended Path for Our Setup

Given your strong PI MCTS/value and belief sampler, a practical progression is:

1. **Baseline PIMC-MCTS:** Start with standard determinized MCTS at the root: sample N hands from your belief, run a few dozen MCTS sims on each (using your PI evaluator), and average action-values.  Use *fixed-action-per-infoset* by merging the trees (i.e. ISMCTS/UCB in one tree).  This is cheapest to implement using your existing MCTS code.  Check if it sharpens values.  _Decision gate:_ If moves still lack separation or performance is no better than your existing PI-net policy, move on.

2. **Single-Observer ISMCTS:** Next, implement SO-ISMCTS (or FPMCTS): maintain one UCT tree of information sets, redeterminizing each simulation (like InfoSet MCTS).  This ensures one action per infoset and avoids fusion.  It will use more memory/iteration but may yield clearer move values.  _Decision gate:_ If MCTS still doesn’t improve move selection (values remain mushy) or costs too much, proceed to next.

3. **Bayesian-inference MCTS:** Incorporate your belief model more strongly: for each sample world, weight its influence by its posterior probability (Bayes) using your policy/value nets.  One approach is: sample worlds, compute their log-prob (from belief + neural policy match), and during tree backup weight returns by these.  This biases search toward more likely deals.  If that helps separate moves, good.  If not, consider increasing N or adjusting belief calibration.

4. **Online Policy Adaptation:** Try Suphx-style adaptation: from the current information state, sample many worlds and run your learned policy (without search) to simulate outcomes, then do a few gradient steps on your policy/value net to specialize to this hand (if possible).  This is equivalent to giving your net a custom training step per round.  It's somewhat heavy, but might produce sharper policies for the specific hand.  If your infrastructure permits quick fine-tuning, this is worth a shot before full RL.

5. **Hybrid Search+CFR (if needed):** If none of the above yield satisfactory play, consider a CFR-based search.  You already have PI evaluation; you could implement a shallow (2-4 ply) *public belief search* using regret-matching at leaves (similar to ReBeL/CFR).  For example, use your MCTS/PI as a roll-in policy but at leaves run a quick CFR tableau (with a linear payoff model) to adjust values.  This is complex but would directly attack the equilibrium.  Only do this if performance truly demands it, due to complexity.

At each stage, the decision gate is simple: **if search yields a clear best move and improves win-rate vs. not-search, keep it**; otherwise move to the next idea.  Also monitor computational cost (time per move).

If all search-based fixes fail, **PTIE** could be a fallback: train a policy with a perfect-information critic (like PerfectDou) and use that policy with no search.  It avoids fusion entirely and might handle “mushy” averaging by learning from the critiqued outcomes.  But it won’t exploit your strong PI MCTS at inference.

## Key Papers – One-line Takeaways

- **Frank & Basin (AAAI 1998):** Identified strategy fusion and non-locality as fundamental flaws of PIMC in imperfect-info games.  
- **Long *et al.* (AAAI 2010):** Analyzed when PIMC succeeds; it often works if leaf utilities are correlated, but can fail otherwise.  
- **Ginsberg (GIB, 1999):** First successful use of PIMC in Bridge, using ~50 sampled deals per decision, plus lattice search to mitigate PIMC errors.  
- **Cowling *et al.* (IEEE TCIAIG 2012):** Introduced Info-Set MCTS (ISMCTS): a single-tree MCTS over information sets that aggregates stats across worlds and eliminates strategy fusion.  
- **Buro *et al.* (2009):** Showed improved inference algorithms for determinization (Skat AI) can greatly boost PIMC performance.  
- **Jiang *et al.* (DeltaDou, IJCAI 2019):** Developed FPMCTS (sample opponent moves, search only for current player) plus Bayesian opponent inference, reaching human-level play in DouDizhu.  
- **Li *et al.* (Suphx, NeurIPS 2020):** Achieved world-class Mahjong by deep RL with novel techniques (global reward pred, oracle guidance, and pMCPA).  
- **Brown *et al.* (ReBeL, NeurIPS 2020):** Introduced ReBeL, which uses belief-state networks and CFR-based search to provably approach Nash equilibrium in 2-player games.  
- **Schmid *et al.* (Student/Player of Games, 2023):** Proposed GT-CFR, a growing-tree CFR search with neural nets, yielding a sound, general algorithm for large perfect and imperfect-information games.  
- **Yang *et al.* (PerfectDou, NeurIPS 2022):** Proposed Perfect-Training-Imperfect-Execution: train an actor-critic with full info, execute with imperfect info (no search) for strong DouDizhu play.  

These sources provide formal definitions, examples, and comparisons of all discussed methods.
